MICH — ME: THE BRAND

Brand source of truth for the personal website and digital identity

Version: 1.0
Primary name: Egbe Michel
Personal name: Mich
Core idea: More than meets the eye.
Personal slogan: “Curious, intentional, and always chasing the next
idea worth bringing to life.”
Design principle: Less, but never empty.

────────

1. Why this document exists

This document defines who Mich is, how the brand should feel, and how
that identity should guide the website. It is written for designers,
developers, and AI coding agents working on the project.

Treat it as the source of truth for brand decisions. Read it before
making substantial visual, copy, layout, or motion changes.

The goal is not to make every page look branded by adding a logo to it.
The goal is to make the whole experience feel like it could only belong
to Mich.

When an existing brand asset is supplied in the project, inspect and use
it. Do not casually redraw, replace, reinterpret, or invent a competing
logo, pattern, or identity element.

2. The person behind the brand

Mich is curious, thoughtful, intentional, funny, a little quirky, and
more warm than he may initially appear. He can come across as serious or
intimidating at first; people who get to know him often discover a
playful, kind, surprising side.

He is drawn to things that are carefully made: Apple products,
mechanical watches, cars, cinema, architecture, visual design,
technology, and objects with a strong sense of form and purpose. He
appreciates engineering and craftsmanship as much as appearance. He
enjoys simplicity when it is the result of thought—not when it is
empty or generic.

He likes ideas that make someone stop and think:

• “That is clever. Why didn’t I think of that?”
• “Even if I had thought of it, would I have executed it like this?”
• “There is more going on here than I noticed at first.”

Mich is not one narrow professional title. He is a person who explores
ideas and brings them to life through different mediums. The brand must
remain valid as his interests, projects, and career evolve.

The defining contrasts

The identity should preserve these tensions rather than choosing one
side:

• Approachable × intimidating
• Technical × artistic
• Serious × funny
• Composed × playful
• Simple at first glance × interesting on closer inspection

Do not turn these contrasts into a gimmick. Let them emerge naturally
through the content, visual system, photography, and details.

3. Brand idea and slogan

Core idea: More than meets the eye.

The first impression should be composed and clear. A closer look should
reveal character, a thoughtful detail, an unexpected interaction, or a
little humor.

This idea should guide the experience, not be repeated as a headline on
every page.

Personal slogan

> Curious, intentional, and always chasing the next idea worth bringing
> to life.

Use this as the principal statement of Mich’s outlook. Preserve its
meaning and wording. Punctuation and line breaks may adapt to a layout,
but do not casually replace it with generic motivational copy.

Design principle: Less, but never empty.

Every element should have a reason to exist. Restraint does not mean
making everything blank, cold, or minimal. The desired result is
edited abundance: enough texture, content, personality, and detail
to feel alive, with nothing that feels random or cluttered.

Before adding an element, ask: 1. Does it communicate something? 2. Does
it improve the experience? 3. Does it feel like Mich? 4. Would removing
it make the result clearer or stronger?

If it has no good reason to be there, remove it.

4. Brand positioning

Present Mich as a curious, multidisciplinary person who notices details,
explores ideas, and cares about how things are made and experienced.

The website is a personal world and selected body of work, not just
a CV, a list of skills, a software developer template, or a digital
agency pretending to be a person.

Technology and software are important parts of Mich’s work, but they are
not the whole identity. Make room for design, experiments, interests,
observations, and things worth sharing. Do not force every interest into
a professional service or every personal detail into a case study.

The site should communicate: - Curiosity: Mich explores, asks
questions, learns, and experiments. - Intentionality: decisions are
considered; details are not arbitrary. - Craft: the result matters,
not just the idea. - Range: different kinds of work can coexist
under one personality. - Humanity: there is a real, funny,
thoughtful person behind the work. - Momentum: Mich is still
learning, making, and following worthwhile ideas.

Do not falsely present Mich as an established agency, a celebrity, a
luxury influencer, or an expert in every field. Confidence should come
from the quality and clarity of the work—not inflated claims.

5. Naming and identity hierarchy

Use the names deliberately:

• Egbe Michel — full name; formal identity and primary wordmark.
• Mich — familiar, personal-facing name; use naturally in the
website’s voice and personal contexts.
• E + M + 21 — secondary symbol designed by Mich; use only the
supplied, approved artwork.

Do not invent a new logo, monogram, or symbol if the project already
contains approved assets. Do not make every occurrence of the name a
logo. Normal text is often more effective.

The number 21 may carry personal significance and appear as a subtle
identity detail where appropriate. Do not scatter it everywhere or
invent a public explanation for it.

6. Visual identity

Core colors

Use these exact core colors unless the supplied brand assets document a
deliberate, approved variation:

Name         HEX         Intended role

────────

Cream        #F3EBDD   Main light background; warmth and breathing room
Dark brown   #2A1E1A   Main text, dark surfaces, visual grounding
Red          #B3262E   Signature accent and deliberate interruption

Cream and dark brown do most of the work. Red is used sparingly so it
feels meaningful when it appears.

Suggested starting balance: roughly 70% cream, 25% dark brown, 5%
red. This is a visual guideline, not a rigid quota. Photography and
supplied brand patterns may shift the balance.

Do not automatically add gradients, gold, neon colors, or a large
secondary palette. If a new color is needed for a specific reason, use
it as a considered exception and check that it works with the core
palette.

Always maintain readable text contrast. Do not sacrifice accessibility
for a color treatment.

Typography

The typographic personality should combine: 1. A refined foundation
— composed and legible. 2. A distinctive display voice — a
little unusual, memorable, and worth looking at twice. 3. A functional
text voice — clear, quiet, and highly readable.

Use the actual fonts already supplied or selected in the project.
Inspect the assets and existing implementation before choosing
replacements. Do not load a collection of competing typefaces just to
make the site look creative.

Use expressive typography for moments that deserve attention. Body copy,
navigation, labels, and controls should remain easy to read. The
contrast between expressive and functional type is intentional.

Logo and pattern

The provided logo artwork and brand pattern are the authority. Use them
consistently and preserve their proportions, spacing, and intended
colors.

• Do not stretch, skew, redraw, or apply arbitrary effects to logo
files.
• Do not use the logo as a substitute for good layout.
• Do not place the pattern behind every section.
• Use the pattern selectively: as a background detail, framing device,
transition, or close-up moment where it adds depth.
• If the asset’s intended use is unclear, inspect the file and compare
variants before making a decision. Do not guess at a new version.

Graphic behavior

The visual system should feel editorial, tactile, and considered, with
occasional unexpected details.

Useful techniques include strong scale contrast, asymmetrical but
balanced layouts, deliberate cropping, generous negative space, small
annotations, thoughtful dividers, and one surprising visual move at a
time.

Avoid decoration without purpose: random blobs, generic abstract
gradients, glassmorphism everywhere, excessive pill-shaped cards, noisy
background shapes, gratuitous borders, and animations that exist only to
prove the site is animated.

7. Photography and imagery

Photography should feel like Quiet Character + Controlled Play.

Prefer natural, candid, human images over constant posing. A photograph
of Mich laughing or absorbed in something can communicate more than a
heavily staged portrait. Images may also reflect his
interests—objects, watches, cars, architecture, cinema, workspaces,
and details he notices.

Image direction: - Natural light and believable environments are
welcome. - A composed, editorial image can sit beside a casual or funny
moment. - Keep color treatment consistent with the site’s warm,
restrained foundation. - Allow occasional unusual crops, close-ups, or
unexpected framing. - Do not force every image to contain the logo or
brand pattern. - Do not use generic stock photography to represent Mich
when a real image or a designed visual would be more truthful. - Do not
fabricate photographs of Mich, his life, credentials, projects, or
achievements. - If the right image is not available, create a layout
that works without it or clearly identify the missing asset.

8. Voice and writing

The voice is intelligent, intentional, curious, slightly witty,
confident without trying too hard, and occasionally playful.

Write like a real person who thinks carefully and has a sense of
humor—not a corporation, a motivational speaker, or an agency selling
itself.

Writing rules

• Prefer specific, plain language over vague claims.
• Be confident without bragging.
• Let humor appear naturally; do not make every sentence a joke.
• Be thoughtful without sounding overly philosophical.
• Keep headlines short when possible.
• Give the reader enough context to understand the work.
• Explain what was interesting, difficult, or worth exploring.
• Never claim results, clients, metrics, experience, or capabilities
that have not been verified.
• Do not fill space with generic phrases such as “passionate
innovator,” “pushing boundaries,” “revolutionizing the future,” or
“where creativity meets technology.”
• Do not force the slogan into every section.
• Avoid describing Mich as mysterious, elite, luxurious, or a genius.
The work should reveal his character without making grand claims
about him.

9. Website experience

The site should feel like entering Mich’s world: clear enough to
navigate immediately, with enough character to reward exploration.

First impression

Within a few seconds, a visitor should understand: - whose site this
is; - the kind of person and maker Mich is; - where to find selected
work; - how to learn more or make contact.

The first screen should make a clear impression without trying to
explain Mich’s entire personality at once.

On closer inspection

The site may reveal more through project stories, small visual details,
playful interactions, photography, side notes, and carefully timed
motion. These details should reward curiosity without making basic
navigation harder.

Content priorities

Prioritize: 1. Identity: a clear introduction to Mich and his point
of view. 2. Selected work: a curated set of real projects,
experiments, and things made. 3. Process and thinking: context about
why a project exists and how decisions were made. 4. Beyond the
work: selected interests or observations that reveal the person. 5.
Contact: an obvious, low-friction way to reach Mich.

Do not add a section merely because other portfolio sites have one.
Every section must have a purpose and real or clearly planned content.

Project presentation

A project should be presented as more than a thumbnail and a list of
technologies. Where information is available, explain: - the problem,
question, or starting point; - Mich’s role and contribution; - the key
decisions or constraints; - the result and what was learned; - relevant
images, demos, repositories, or links.

Be honest about unfinished work, team contributions, prototypes, and
experimental outcomes. Do not invent impact metrics or imply sole
ownership of collaborative work.

Interaction and motion

Motion should express character, establish hierarchy, reveal
information, or make an interaction feel better. It should not slow down
the visitor or compete with the content.

• Use purposeful, short transitions.
• Keep navigation and controls predictable.
• Respect prefers-reduced-motion.
• Avoid long intro sequences that prevent people from reaching the
work.
• Avoid cursor effects, scroll hijacking, and parallax when they make
the site less usable.
• Make touch interactions work well on mobile.
• Keep performance, accessibility, and responsive behavior as
requirements—not afterthoughts.

10. The Two26 Studio reference

Reference: https://two26.studio/

Use Two26 as inspiration for the quality of thinking and execution,
not as a visual template.

What is worth learning from: - A clear point of view instead of generic
agency language. - Typography and composition that carry personality. -
Work presented with context and a strong visual hierarchy. - A brand
voice that is professional but still human and witty. - A flexible
system that supports different projects without making everything
identical. - Motion and art direction used to give the experience
character. - A belief that attractive design should also have a reason
behind it.

What must not happen: - Do not copy its layouts, wording, brand assets,
animations, project presentation, or distinctive visual devices. - Do
not turn Mich’s site into an imitation of a design studio. - Do not
borrow its jokes or slogans. - Do not add agency services, team
sections, or client language that does not fit Mich. - Do not assume
that “more effects” means “more creative.”

Translate the lesson into Mich’s own identity: a personal site with
studio-level thoughtfulness and execution, but unmistakably a
person—not an agency.

11. Non-negotiable design rules

1. The person comes before the profession. Technology is part of
Mich, not his entire identity.
2. Use the supplied brand assets. Inspect the project before
creating substitutes.
3. Cream and dark brown lead; red is an accent.
4. Less, but never empty. Avoid both sterile minimalism and
clutter.
5. Make the first glance clear and the second glance rewarding.
6. Use expressive details selectively. One strong idea is better
than five competing effects.
7. Humanity matters. The site should not feel cold, arrogant, or
self-important.
8. The work must be truthful. Never invent projects, photos,
testimonials, metrics, or credentials.
9. Content leads design. Do not build elaborate empty sections to
fill space.
10. Mobile is a first-class experience. The site must be excellent
on a phone, not merely shrink down from desktop.
11. Accessibility and performance are part of craft.
12. Do not over-brand every pixel. A plain photograph, a quiet page,
or a simple link can belong to the identity without a logo stamped
on it.

12. Website agent instructions

Before changing the site:

1. Inspect the existing codebase, routes, components, styling system,
and package configuration.
2. Locate and inspect the supplied logo files, brand pattern, fonts,
photography, and other assets.
3. Read this document fully.
4. Identify what already works and what should be improved; do not
rewrite the whole application without a reason.
5. Make a concise implementation plan that maps the current site to
this brand direction.

During implementation:

• Preserve working functionality and existing project conventions
unless there is a clear reason to change them.
• Reuse existing components where appropriate; build reusable
components where they genuinely reduce repetition.
• Use the existing framework and dependencies. Do not add a library
just to achieve a decorative effect.
• Do not invent asset paths. Confirm the actual files and import
paths.
• Do not replace supplied logos or patterns with text approximations.
• Do not add placeholder copy that looks like a finished claim.
• Do not create sections that have no content or clear purpose.
• Do not copy Two26’s design.
• Check responsive behavior, keyboard navigation, contrast, reduced
motion, loading states, and basic performance.
• Keep the code maintainable; avoid a single enormous component and
avoid unnecessary abstractions.

After implementation:

• Run the available lint, type-check, test, and build commands where
practical.
• Fix issues introduced by the changes.
• Summarize what changed, which assets were used, which checks passed,
and what content or assets are still needed.
• Clearly flag any decision that requires Mich’s approval instead of
silently inventing a brand rule.

14. How to decide whether a design is right

Before approving a page or component, ask:

• Does this feel like Mich rather than a generic portfolio?
• Is it clear what the visitor can do next?
• Does the design feel considered rather than decorated?
• Is there personality without noise?
• Is there enough warmth and humanity?
• Is the unexpected detail earned?
• Does the page work on a phone?
• Is every factual claim true?
• Would this still feel appropriate if Mich’s career direction
changed?
• Does it feel like more than meets the eye without having to
announce that phrase?

If the result is beautiful but generic, it is not finished.
If it is clever but confusing, it is not finished.
If it is minimal but lifeless, it is not finished.
If it is expressive but exhausting, it is not finished.

The target is a personal identity that feels considered, curious,
human, and memorable.

Final direction: Build a website that makes people curious about
Mich, gives them meaningful work to explore, and leaves them with the
feeling that there is more to discover. Let the design show intention.
Let the details reward attention. Let the person remain visible.
